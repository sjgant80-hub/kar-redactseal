What it is, in plain words

Redaction is everywhere there is a duty to disclose but also to protect: a court orders documents released with third-party names blacked out; a freedom-of-information request is answered with exempt passages removed; a company shares an incident report with a partner but hides a customer's details. The person receiving the redacted document has a reasonable worry: was anything else quietly changed? And the person who redacted has a matching worry: if I later have to reveal one of the hidden parts, can I prove it is the real one and not something I swapped in?

Today both sides rely on trust and a careful re-read. There is no small, portable proof that travels with the document.

RedactSeal is that proof. You give it the document and the spans to remove. It returns the redacted text — each removal replaced by a numbered marker [[R0]], [[R1]]… — a list of positions (where each removal sat and how long it was), and a single seal: a hash chain that binds the exact removed content to its exact place. Anyone holding the original can confirm the released redaction matches the sealed original. And later, if a removed span is disclosed, anyone holding the redacted document and the seal can check the disclosure is faithful — right content, right length, right position — without being shown the other removals. A wrong reveal, even one the same length, fails. The code a mutation gate proves is the code running under your clicks.

Honest limits — read this before you trust it

The hash is FNV-1a, a fast 32-bit non-cryptographic hash — not SHA-256. It is here because it is tiny, dependency-free, and reproducible for the mutation gate. Against an accident or an honest mistake it is more than enough: a changed character, a wrong reveal, a misplaced span all change the hash chain and fail verification. Against a determined adversary with collision-finding effort, a 32-bit hash is forgeable — someone could, with work, craft a different removed content that produces the same seal.

What is real, and what to swap. The asset here is the verification logic, proven correct by a 32/32 mutation gate: it checks that every marker is present, every revealed span has exactly its pinned length, every revealed span sits at exactly its claimed position, and the recomputed hash chain equals the seal. That logic does not change when you change the hash. For adversarial use, replace the one fnv1a function with SHA-256 (or any collision-resistant hash) and keep everything else — the structure, the chain, and the proven checks. It is the honest-faithfulness proof and the verification engine, with a swappable hash primitive, not a finished cryptographic redaction product out of the box.

How it works — traced from a real run

Take the sample with all four fields redacted. The seal of this exact document and these exact removals is 506186502 (hex 1e2bcb06). Here is the lifecycle — the numbers below are the actual run.

A same-length swap is the hard case — length checks alone miss it. The hash chain binds the exact content of every removal to its place, so £82,000 → £99,000 changes the chain and fails. The mutation gate attacks every edge of that check — the length comparison, the marker-present test, the position re-slice, the chain equality — and each of the 32 rewrites is caught by a test.

The kernelkernel.mjsFour pure functions: fnv1a (the swappable hash), seal (redact + chain), verifyOriginal (the original-holder's check), verifyReveal (the disclosure check). No I/O. Bad input returns {ok:false, why}; a failed verification returns {ok:true, valid:false, why} — a verdict, not an error.

The gatewitness.mjsMutates the kernel 32 ways and demands every mutant die under the tests. A survivor means a blind spot in the proof. Re-run on every push.
The proof.github/workflows/gate.ymlCI re-runs tests + gate on GitHub's own runner. Green there is the un-forgeable proof the verification behaves as this page claims.
This pageindex.htmlInlines the kernel byte-for-byte, so the logic you audit in CI is the logic running under your clicks. A parity test fails CI if the two drift.

Why a chain, not one hash of the whole thing? Because the chain binds each removal to its position in sequence as well as its content — so a reveal cannot be shuffled, duplicated, or moved to another slot without breaking it. Each link is hash(previous | start | end | content).

For Legal / e-Discovery

Produce redacted documents with a seal attached. Opposing counsel or the court can confirm nothing outside the redactions was altered, and a later court-ordered un-redaction of a specific passage can be proven faithful — without exposing the other protected passages. For FOI / Information Governance

Answer a request with exempt passages removed and a seal that the requester can check. If an exemption is later overturned and a passage released, the seal proves the released text is exactly what was there — closing the gap where a requester suspects the response was quietly edited.

For the DPO / Privacy

When sharing a document that mentions third parties, redact their data and keep the seal. It demonstrates data-minimisation was applied without altering the record, and lets you prove, if challenged, that the redaction was faithful — an audit artefact for a subject-access or disclosure dispute.

For the CTO / Eng lead

One pure function, zero dependencies, no network. Drop it into a document pipeline, a disclosure portal, or an agent that prepares releases. It never throws; a bad span returns a clean refusal. Swap the hash for SHA-256 where you need adversarial strength — the proven logic is unchanged.

White paper — the short version

The problem. A redacted document asks its recipient to trust that only the blacked-out parts were removed, and asks the redactor to be able to prove, later, that a disclosed part is the genuine one. Neither side has a small, portable artefact that settles it; both fall back on a manual re-read and good faith.

The claim. A redaction should carry a seal that binds the exact removed content to its exact position, so that (a) the holder of the original can confirm the released redaction is faithful, and (b) any later disclosure of a removed span can be checked against the seal — right content, right length, right position — without the verifier seeing the other removals. RedactSeal computes this as a hash chain, each link hash(previous | start | end | content), so a reveal cannot be altered, shortened, moved or reordered without breaking it.

Why a mutation gate, not just tests. Passing tests prove the code works on the cases you thought of. A mutation gate proves the tests work: it rewrites the kernel 32 ways — weakening the length check, dropping the marker test, skipping the position re-slice, loosening the chain equality — and demands the tests catch every one. 32/32 killed means the verification has no blind spot a forgery could slip through.

What it is not — stated plainly. The shipped hash is FNV-1a, a 32-bit non-cryptographic function, chosen for size and reproducibility. It is robust against accident and honest error, and the verification logic it drives is proven correct; it is not collision-resistant against a determined adversary. Replace fnv1a with SHA-256 for that, keeping the chain and the proven checks. RedactSeal is the faithful-redaction proof structure and verification engine with a swappable hash — not a sealed cryptographic product as shipped. It also does not find the sensitive spans for you (that is your policy or a detector upstream); it proves the redaction you chose is faithful.

Risks, answered

"The hash could be forged by an attacker."True of FNV-1a (32-bit) — swap in SHA-256; the proven logic is unchanged.
"It might miss sensitive text."Out of scope — it proves the spans you chose are faithfully removed; pair with a detector/policy for what to remove.
"Someone reveals the wrong content."Caught — wrong length, wrong position, or a changed character all fail verification.
"The code could change silently."CI re-runs the gate + parity; a weakened check fails 32/32 or breaks parity.
